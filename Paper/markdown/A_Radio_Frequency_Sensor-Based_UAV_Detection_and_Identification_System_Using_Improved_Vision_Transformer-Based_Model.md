# A Radio Frequency Sensor-Based UAV Detection and Identification System Using Improved Vision Transformer-Based Model
Lu Xu, Rui Shi, and Yijia Zhang

***Abstract*—In this article, we propose a novel approach to addressing the challenge of unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) detection and identification by combining object detection and image classification techniques. Diverging from prevailing research that predominantly relies on convolutional neural networks (CNNs), we introduce the radio frequency drone vision transformer (RFDroneViT), a novel two-stage vision transformer (ViT) consisting of two distinct sub-ViT. The first stage employs the designed end-to-end object detection with transformer (DETR) to detect the UAV, while the second stage utilizes ViT-B/16 to identify the detected UAV. Based on RFDroneViT, we propose several detailed optimization algorithms, culminating in the development of a cost-effective system capable of UAV detection and identification. We evaluate the system’s performance through rigorous experimentation. Our custom DETR model achieves the state-of-the-art performance in drone signal detection with an average precision (AP) of 74.3 and demonstrates an 8.7 improvement in small target AP (APs) for the signal detection task compared to the original DETR. Additionally, the classification model can achieve 98.7% Top-1. The experimental results demonstrated that our proposed system achieves great performance on drone detection and identification.**

***Index Terms*— Improved designed end-to-end object detection with transformer (DETR)-based model, radio frequency (RF)-based anti-unmanned aerial vehicle (UAV) system, spectrogram sensing technology, vision transformer (ViT).**

In this article, we choose a high-performance computational server equipped with the radio frequency drone vision transformer (RFDroneViT) as the designated processor. The receiver is a USRP due to its renowned versatility and reliability. The RF data collected by the USRP are stored as binary data streams, ensuring efficient and accurate data retention.

The utilization of an RF-based method for drone detection relies on the identification of patterns within the amplitude fluctuations present in the raw signal data. The direct identification of these implicit patterns poses significant challenges. Hence, the explicit mapping of features derived from the raw data plays a pivotal role in determining the system’s implementation complexity. The raw signal is subjected to a short-time Fourier transform (STFT) in this study. The following equation shows the STFT transformation:

$$
\text{STFT}(X) = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x(t) \cdot w(t-\tau) \cdot e^{-j2\pi ft} dt. \quad (1)
$$

Here, $x(t)$ presents the raw signal data, $w(\tau)$ presents the window function, and $f$ presents the frequency. The STFT output is visually depicted through a color-coded representation.

By transforming the binary data stream into the time-frequency domain, we can effectively capture its dynamic characteristics, thereby facilitating detection and classification with enhanced accuracy and robustness. A portion of the spectrogram results in the dataset are presented in Appendix I. As evident from these spectrograms, the inherent characteristics in the raw data exhibit variations attributed to diversity due to different communication protocols employed between the drone and its controller. For example, DJI’s second-generation communication protocol, BigZee, supports Phantom, Matrice, and Inspire series drones. The following equation is the modulation of the uplink control signals, which utilizes frequency hopping spread spectrum (FHSS) technology:

$$
f(t) = A \sum_{k=-\infty}^{+\infty} W_T(t-kT_h) \cos[2\pi f_k(t-kT_h) + \Phi_h]. \quad (2)
$$

Here, $W_T$ represents a rectangular window function with a width of $T_h$. A denotes the modulation amplitude, $f_0, f_1, \dots, f_k (k = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N-1)$ is a set of equidistant frequency points, and $\phi_n (n = 0, 1, 2, \dots, N-1)$ is a set of equidistant initial phase values. Equation (2) indicates that different types of drones exhibit variations in their frequency points and the spacing between these points. Furthermore, the modulation method of frequency hopping (FH) signals is fixed after the drones are manufactured. Formally, this approach is also analogous to directly recognizing drone fingerprints.

The drone detection method proposed in this article primarily operates by identifying various drone signals present within the spectrum. These signals can be distinguished based on their individual transmission durations and bandwidths. Our research and experimentation have revealed that certain video transmission signals typically exhibit a duration of approximately 0.01 s and a bandwidth of around 20 MHz. Due to their relatively large presence in the spectrogram, these signals are more easily detectable for models employed in this study. For this task, we refer to these signals as “conventional signals.”

Conversely, a multitude of frequency-hopping signals, characterized by a duration of approximately 0.001 s and a bandwidth of about 2 MHz, exhibit condensed and frequent appearances within the spectrogram while occupying a smaller spatial extent. In this study, we classify these signals as “small target signals.”

Our experimental findings indicate that detecting such diminutive signals presents greater challenges for model detection compared to conventional signals, thereby resulting in an elevated probability of missed detections.

The detection of small targets has long posed a formidable challenge in traditional object detection tasks. Prominent strategies to tackle this issue encompass enhancing feature maps for small objects,, incorporating contextual information pertaining to small objects, addressing the imbalance between foreground and background classes for small objects, and augmenting training examples specifically tailored for small objects.

In this article, we employ a multilevel feature map fusion approach derived from the FPN to enhance the model’s ability to detect small target signals, effectively solving this challenge.

The structure of RFDroneViT is illustrated in Fig. 3. The first stage of RFDroneViT encompasses *spectrogram windowing*, where the entire spectrogram is divided into several subspectrograms of identical resolution, and *signal detection*, wherein these subspectrograms are sequentially fed into an improved DETR model for the detection of drone signals.

### A. First Stage: Signal Detection Based on Improved DETR Model

Fundamentally, the first-stage model involves an object detection task within the field of computer vision. But why choose DETR among the plethora of excellent object detection networks? This choice is validated by our experimental results, as detailed in Table I.

Theoretically, the effectiveness of this approach is intrinsically linked to the architecture of DETR. DETR employs ViT for object detection, functioning as a comprehensive end-to-end detector. In contrast to traditional two-stage networks such as Faster-RCNN, DETR provides enhanced consistency in processing. Lightweight networks like YOLO encounter difficulties in detecting multiple signal targets within a single image, often leading to missed detections. The deeper architecture of DETR facilitates the effective handling of complex patterns and variations in signal detection, thereby mitigating this problem.

Furthermore, the end-to-end design of DETR eliminates the need for hand-crafted anchor boxes and nonmaximum suppression, simplifying the detection pipeline and improving robustness. This structural advantage, combined with its ability to learn global context through self-attention mechanisms, enhances its performance in diverse and challenging environments.

However, DETR exhibits suboptimal performance in recognizing small-signal targets. The primary reason stems from its reliance on the backbone for feature extraction. The inefficiency of the backbone network to extract features from input images has a detrimental impact on the detection of small targets. A^{\text{MSA}}_{m,k} = \exp \left( \frac{X^{OQ} U_m^T V_m x^{OQ}_k}{\sqrt{C_v}} \right) \quad (11)
$$

### B. Second Stage: UAV Classification Based on ViT

In the field of signal processing, extensive research has been conducted utilizing CNN to perform a variety of classification tasks, including signal modulation style classification. These approaches either employ CNN to classify raw data directly or to classify feature representations derived from the raw data. Building on this body of work, this article leverages the distinctive time-frequency representations of different UAVs to achieve UAV classification.

Traditional CNN-based image classification networks heavily rely on the convolutional layers to extract features from images. While convolution operations can accurately extract pixel-level features, the interconnection between each convolution kernel is relatively weak. This issue is exacerbated in low signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) conditions, where the difference in pixel values between signal and background noise is often minimal. We show the drone signals under different SNR spectrograms in Appendix III.

Therefore, we selected the ViT-B/16 model as our second-stage model. The entire structure of the ViT-B/16 model is shown in Fig. 5.

First, we segment the subwindows $X_{sw} \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times H \times W}$ obtained from the first-stage model into smaller patches $x_p \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times (P^2 \cdot 3)}$, where (H × W) is the resolution of the original image, P is the resolution of the small patches, and $N = (HW/P^2)$ is the number of small patches. Subsequently, we embedded these patches using a layer with hidden units of size D and applied positional encoding, transforming the small patches into input vectors $X_0 \in \mathbb{R}^{N \times D}$ for the encoder. The following equation utilized W-MSA to compute these feature vectors L times:

$$
X_l = W_l \cdot \text{LN}[\text{W-MSA}\{\text{LN}(X_{l-1})\}]. \quad (14)
$$

Here, $W_l$ represents the multilayer perceptron (MLP) weights connected to the lth encoder. LN and W-MSA are the same mechanisms used in Section III-A. The following equations show that the final feature vectors are input into a linear layer to output the probability distribution:

$$
x_{\text{class}} = W_{\text{Linear}} \cdot X_L \quad (15)

By applying the softmax function in (16), we transform the inference results from tensor into probability of each class. Transfer learning alleviates the computational burden for models to comprehend intricate data.

To facilitate the model’s convergence on a large dataset, we employed a warm-up training strategy in the initial stages. Specifically, the model was trained with a gradually increasing learning rate starting from a lower value over several epochs, which mitigated potential instability during early training phases. Furthermore, we implemented a cosine annealing learning rate scheduler alongside an increased batch size. This combination enabled the model to dynamically adjust the learning rate according to the training progress, thereby enhancing the effectiveness of fine-tuning.

### C. System Detail Explanation

*Why Windowing Before First-Stage Model?* Commercial drones, like DJI, often have automatic and manual communication frequency selection modes. When the pilot uses the automatic mode, the flight controller selects a frequency band with less noise for communication automatically. In such scenarios, it is necessary to monitor a wide spectrogram band. This results in the model will search signal in a massive spectrogram. Directly feeding such spectrogram into the first-stage model is impossible due to the high compute complexity $O(4hwC^2 + 2h^2w^2C)$. Therefore, we window the entire spectrogram into smaller windows before feeding it into the model in the first stage. This operation standardizes the size of the input windows for the model and reduces the computational complexity to $O(hwC^2) + O((h^2w^2/M^2)C)$. The results of our experiments demonstrate that by utilizing the 3090 GPU for inference and setting M = 2, the average inference time on a 7400 × 800 resolution spectrogram (1850 × 800 after windowing) decreases from 376.81 to 243.79 ms (60.9475 × 4). Consequently, this leads to a notable reduction in processing time of approximately 36% compared to methodologies that do not incorporate windowing techniques. For calculation details, see Appendix II.

*Why Window Filtering Before The Second-Stage Model?* The window filtering algorithm is a postprocessing step for the first-stage signal detection results. It reduces the number of empty windows passed to the second-stage model, thereby decreasing its processing time. Additionally, it addresses the issue of signal splitting caused by windowing, where a signal might be divided between two adjacent windows. This split can distort the signal shape, especially for wideband signals, leading to inaccurate drone-type recognition. To resolve these issues, the following equations described a new window filtering algorithm:

$$
y_{\text{Avg}} = \frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N} (y_1 + y_2 + \dots + y_n)}{N} \quad (17)
$$

$$
\text{Coor}_{w1} = (x_w, y_{\text{Avg}}) \quad (18)
$$

$$
\text{Coor}_{w2} = (x_w, y_{\min}) \quad (19)
$$

$$
\text{Coor}_{w3} = (x_w, y_{\max}). \quad (20)
$$

Here, N is the number of detected FHSS signals in a single subwindow. $y_n$ represents the frequency band (y-axis) of the nth FHSS signal. $y_{\text{Avg}}$ is the average frequency point of all FHSS signals within the current window. $x_w$ is the central time slot (x-axis) of the current window. In (18)–(20), we derive extra three predicted window center coordinates, $\text{Coor}_{w1}$, $\text{Coor}_{w2}$, and $\text{Coor}_{w3}$. These coordinates use fixed window lengths ($l_w$) and heights ($h_w$) to generate three extra subwindows, including the lower and upper parts of the origin subwindow. The proposed design ensures that at least one of the generated windows encompasses the entire drone signal, thereby mitigating the miss rate.

## APPENDIX II

### COMPUTATIONAL COMPLEXITY FOR WINDOWING

In this section, we demonstrate how the spectrogram windowing operation can reduce computational complexity. To provide a clear comparison, we first calculate the computational complexity of the original multihead self-attention (MSA) mechanism in the Swin Transformer. First assume the following.

1) The height and width of the input feature map are h and w, respectively.
2) The number of channels is C.
3) The number of positions (tokens) is N = hw.

Equations (21)–(24) are the overall computational complexity of MSA.

First, we should perform the linear transformations. Before the self-attention, the input features are linearly transformed to generate the query (Q), key (K), and value (V). Each linear transformation has a complexity of

$$
\text{Linear Transform:} O(hwC \times C) = O(hwC^2). \quad (21)
$$

Since there are three linear transformations (one for each of Q, K, and V), the total complexity is $O(3hwC^2)$. Additionally, there is a final linear transformation after concatenating the multihead outputs $O(hwC^2)$. Thus, the total complexity for linear transformations is

$$
O(3hwC^2) + O(hwC^2) = O(4hwC^2). \quad (22)
$$

Then, we perform attention weights and weighted sum. For each head, the dot product between the Q and K needs to be computed. Assuming there are M heads, and each head has a dimension $d_k = C/M$, the complexity of computing QKᵀ is

$$
O(hw \times hw \times d_k) = O \left( h^2w^2 \times \frac{C}{M} \right) \quad (23)
$$

Since there are M heads, the total complexity is $O(h^2w^2C)$.
